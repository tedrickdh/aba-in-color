ABA Unfiltered: What Happens When the Textbook Meets Real Life?

From the ABA in Color conversation: This educational article expands on a past event topic using behavior-analytic concepts and published sources. It is not a transcript or a claim about statements made by individual speakers.

The graph suggests improvement. The caregiver says the week has been harder than ever. The technician sees something else. Who is right?

Maybe everyone is reporting something important. Real-life behavior is complex, and one measure rarely captures every part of a person's experience.

Start with the behavior—not the label

Terms like “noncompliant,” “attention-seeking,” or “unmotivated” can hide more than they explain. A behavior-analytic approach asks what someone did, under what conditions, and what happened afterward. Operational definitions and direct measurement help teams talk about the same events.

A graph is a tool, not the whole story

Frequency, duration, latency, and other measures can be valuable, but each answers a different question. A reduction in one measured response does not automatically mean that quality of life has improved. Teams should also consider meaningful outcomes, social validity, client assent where applicable, and the perspectives of people affected by services (Schwartz & Baer, 1991; Wolf, 1978).

Different observers see different contexts

A caregiver’s report and a clinician’s session data may differ because the environments, routines, and consequences differ. Instead of deciding who is mistaken, explore those contextual variables. Ask what happens before and after the behavior, what changed recently, and whether the measurement system is sensitive to what matters.

When the first hypothesis does not hold

Functional assessment is a process of developing and testing hypotheses, not attaching a permanent label to a person (Hanley, Iwata, & McCord, 2003). When new information conflicts with a hypothesis, good practice is to revisit the assessment, review data quality, and adjust the plan with appropriate clinical oversight.

Questions worth bringing to the table

  1. Are we measuring a behavior that matters to the learner and family?
  2. What contextual variables might explain different reports?
  3. What evidence would change our current interpretation?
  4. Whose voice is missing from the decision?

Takeaway: Good science is not threatened by hard questions. It gets stronger when professionals remain curious, collaborative, and willing to revise their assumptions.

Let’s talk

When have multiple perspectives helped you rethink a behavior hypothesis? Keep stories hypothetical or fully de-identified.

References and further reading

  • Hanley, G. P., Iwata, B. A., & McCord, B. E. (2003). Functional analysis of problem behavior: A review. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 36(2), 147–185.
  • Schwartz, I. S., & Baer, D. M. (1991). Social validity assessments: Is current practice state of the art? Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 24(2), 189–204.
  • Wolf, M. M. (1978). Social validity: The case for subjective measurement or how applied behavior analysis is finding its heart. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 11(2), 203–214.

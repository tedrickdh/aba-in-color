Copy, Paste & Consequences: When Clinical Documentation Stops Telling the Truth

From the ABA in Color conversation: This educational article expands on a past event topic using behavior-analytic concepts and published sources. It is not a transcript or a claim about statements made by individual speakers.

Two sessions. Two different days. Nearly identical notes. The client made progress. The intervention worked. But did those things happen—or did someone just copy yesterday’s story?

In applied behavior analysis, documentation is more than paperwork. It is how we communicate observations, evaluate treatment, and support decisions about services.

Professional language is not the same as evidence

Baer, Wolf, and Risley (1968) described ABA through seven dimensions: applied, behavioral, analytic, technological, conceptually systematic, effective, and generality. Those dimensions remind us to describe behavior in observable terms and connect conclusions to evidence.

Consider the phrase “the client demonstrated improved compliance.” Which behavior improved? Under what conditions? Compared with what baseline? What data support that statement? A useful note distinguishes observation from interpretation and identifies the relevant skills, prompts, and responses.

Is copying always wrong?

No. Standardized language for established procedures and carefully designed templates can improve consistency. The problem begins when repeated text claims observations, services, or outcomes that were not actually documented for the session in question. Each record needs to reflect the service provided and the information available that day. The concern is not unique to ABA: in medicine, copying text forward in electronic health records has been flagged as a threat to record accuracy for two decades (Hirschtick, 2006).

Documentation itself is behavior

Behavior analysts also need to examine the environment in which staff write notes. Tight deadlines, high caseloads, unclear examples, and feedback focused only on speed may influence how notes are completed. That does not excuse inaccurate records. It gives supervisors additional variables to assess and improve.

Are staff given time to document? Do they know what objective descriptions look like? Does feedback reinforce accuracy and meaningful detail, not just speed?

Ethics is more than a checkbox

The BACB Ethics Code for Behavior Analysts speaks to this directly. Standard 1.01, Being Truthful, requires behavior analysts to provide truthful and accurate information and to correct inaccurate submissions as soon as they become aware of them. Standard 3.11, Documenting Professional Activity, requires detailed, high-quality documentation throughout the service relationship. Applicable requirements also differ by credential, setting, payer, and organization. A note should not state that an intervention occurred when it did not, or report progress without a reasonable basis.

Three questions before you submit

  1. Does this record describe what happened in this specific session?
  2. Could another qualified professional understand the meaningful events and outcomes?
  3. Can I explain the observations or data behind my conclusions?

Takeaway: The goal is not a perfect-sounding note. It is an accurate, useful record that supports ethical decisions and better care.
